# Literature Landscape Review

Build an auditable literature map and a defensible contribution claim. Optimize for finding the strongest counterexample to novelty, not for confirming the user's initial idea.

## Route the task

- For a field or direction survey, read [references/search-and-coverage.md](references/search-and-coverage.md).
- For novelty checking, idea evaluation, or contribution positioning, read [references/novelty-and-contribution.md](references/novelty-and-contribution.md).
- For research-direction decisions, paper proposals, or requests that combine both, read both references.

## Non-negotiable principles

1. Treat coverage as bounded and auditable, never as absolute. State the search cutoff, sources or search surfaces, query families, inclusion boundaries, and residual blind spots.
2. For recent or fast-moving work, use live scholarly or web search. If live search is unavailable, say that the recent-literature and novelty conclusions are provisional; do not claim that the search is comprehensive or that no prior work exists.
3. Treat novelty as multidimensional rather than binary. Prior work on the broad direction narrows the permissible claim; it does not automatically invalidate the project.
4. Separate verified facts, synthesis, and recommendations. Attach citations to factual claims and label inferences as such.
5. Search adversarially. Actively look for papers that would most weaken the proposed novelty claim, including work under different terminology or in adjacent communities.
6. Never invent a paper or bibliographic detail. Verify title, authors, year, venue or status, and a stable identifier or URL before relying on it. Merge preprint and published versions instead of double-counting them.

## Core workflow

1. **Frame the scope.** Identify the research question, communities, time range, publication types, and intended decision. When details are absent, choose reasonable assumptions and state them.
2. **Model the concept space.** Expand the user's wording into aliases, historical terms, component methods, neighboring problems, application terms, and likely evaluation language.
3. **Run layered retrieval.** Search canonical work, recent work, and adversarially similar work. Then expand through citations, related-paper links, authors, venues, and adjacent fields as described in the coverage reference.
4. **Build an evidence ledger.** Record what each source actually establishes, its publication status, which query path found it, and how it affects the emerging map or contribution claim.
5. **Find the closest prior work.** Do not stop at representative papers. Compare the idea against the nearest papers across problem, assumptions, mechanism, data or feedback, setting, evaluation, and claimed outcome.
6. **Map the contribution boundary.** Distinguish what is established, crowded but still improvable, plausibly differentiating, and speculative. Replace blanket rejection with a conditional statement such as: “Not defensible as X; potentially defensible as Y if Z is demonstrated.”
7. **Connect novelty to value.** Require a plausible mechanism, a consequential difference, and an evidence plan. A new combination, setting, or benchmark is a contribution only when it changes capability, understanding, reliability, efficiency, or another meaningful outcome.
8. **Stop transparently.** Use explicit saturation checks, report unresolved searches and access limitations, and calibrate confidence to the evidence actually gathered.

## Required deliverable

Adapt detail to the request, but preserve these decision-relevant elements:

- Scope, search date, time cutoff, publication-status policy, and method.
- A taxonomy or chronological map of the area, including canonical and recent work.
- A representative-work table with verified citations and each work's actual contribution.
- A closest-prior-work comparison, not just a general related-work list.
- A contribution-boundary assessment: established, weakly differentiating, defensible if validated, and high-risk/speculative aspects.
- Concrete experiments, analyses, or artifacts needed to prove both distinctiveness and practical or scientific value.
- Search blind spots, uncertain claims, and the next highest-value searches.

When the user asks for a short answer, compress the presentation rather than omitting the closest-work comparison, confidence calibration, or caveats.

## Disallowed shortcuts

- Do not infer “no prior work” from failure to find an exact keyword match.
- Do not declare an entire direction unsuitable merely because related work exists.
- Do not call an A+B combination novel without explaining the new mechanism or consequential behavior.
- Do not confuse application novelty, engineering effort, benchmark novelty, and methodological novelty.
- Do not use a numeric novelty score without exposing the evidence and dimensions behind it.
- Do not cite search snippets, generated summaries, or secondary lists as if they were the primary paper.
